STUDENT-BUILT TRAINING RESEARCH PROTOTYPE

Review your swing with racket motion
and body tracking.

SquashSense is a prototype I built to look at a squash swing from two sides at once. A small motion sensor on the end of the racket grip records how fast the racket moves and turns. A camera on a small computer at the side of the court tracks the body and estimates joint angles. The software puts both on one timeline, so after a session you can look at a swing with numbers and video together instead of relying only on what you or a coach remember.

02 /HOW IT WORKS

Three parts,
one timeline.

Everything runs on the local network: the sensor sends its data wirelessly to a receiver plugged into the courtside computer, the camera connects to the same computer, and the training interface is a web page opened on a laptop on the same network. What each part measures or computes comes first; component names come after.

500Hz

Racket sensor

A short cylinder at the end of the grip holds an ESP32-S3 microcontroller board and an ICM42688 six-axis inertial measurement unit (IMU: a three-axis accelerometer plus a three-axis gyroscope). It measures acceleration and rotation rate 500 times a second (the configured rate; during the recorded session the receiver counted about 504 Hz) and sends packets over ESP-NOW, a short-range Wi-Fi-based protocol, to a USB receiver on the computer.

MEASURES DIRECTLY: ACCELERATION · ROTATION RATE
33points

Body tracking

A camera connected to the courtside computer. Google’s MediaPipe Pose model finds 33 body landmarks in each frame; SquashSense’s own code turns those points into the joint-angle labels, the wrist path and the phase chips. In the recording the camera was processed at 14–16 frames per second.

LANDMARKS: MEDIAPIPE (GOOGLE) · ANGLES AND PHASES: SQUASHSENSE
ROCK 5C

Courtside computer

A Radxa ROCK 5C single-board computer in a purple enclosure, with the camera fixed to its front, held to the court glass by a suction mount. It runs the pose model, receives the sensor stream, detects swings, aligns the two clocks and serves the web interface.

How a swing is detected

The software looks for sustained acceleration of the racket rather than a fixed speed threshold (an earlier version used thresholds and missed slow, compact swings). Contact is marked when a shock appears on the accelerometer while the racket is still near peak speed. The phases — backswing, acceleration, contact, follow-through, recovery — are assigned afterwards from the moments where the racket changes direction.

How the two streams are aligned

The sensor, the computer and the browser each keep their own clock, and wireless packets arrive late or go missing. The software aligns the clocks on the fastest delivery it has observed, nudges the offset gradually as the clocks drift, and shows the remaining uncertainty in the interface instead of hiding it.

Both paragraphs are my own description from the project introduction video. This page did not independently inspect the application and firmware code, so treat them as the author’s account rather than third-party-verified implementation details.

03 /TESTING AND LIMITATIONS

What has been tested,
and what hasn’t.

SquashSense is a working prototype, not a validated measurement system. This section keeps “the demonstration worked” separate from “the measurement is right”.

Sessions so far

Development started on simulated data, followed by a small number of informal court sessions with the real sensor stream. Only one session was recorded in full: 27 August 2026, free hitting, 22 minutes, one player (me), 197 swings detected by the software. The sessions were not logged, so no count is quoted here.

Reference method

None. No radar gun, motion-capture system or manually labelled video was used, so no accuracy figure exists for any metric. The one quantity with an independent check is the sample rate: configured at 500 Hz, counted at about 504 Hz by the receiver.

Aligning video and sensor

As I describe it: the clocks are aligned on the fastest observed delivery, corrected gradually as they drift, and the leftover uncertainty is displayed. In the recording the status chip read "sync 89%, offset 0 ms" (同步 89% · 偏移 0 ms), and the synchronized timeline puts gyroscope, accelerometer, phase and contact under one cursor. The actual timing error between a video frame and the sensor peak has not been measured.

Problems visible in the recording

  • The “data latency” card reads about 1,252,800 ms for the whole session. That is the offset between the sensor’s clock reference and the computer’s, not a real delivery delay; this build does not subtract it.
  • The swing-speed card shows 261.7 km/h (+579%) at one point, which is not plausible for a racket; the conversion needs checking.
  • Early in the session the consistency score reads 0 with CV 174%; with few swings the score is meaningless.
  • No neutral pose was recorded (the status chip reads "neutral pose not set", 未设置中立姿态), so orientation-based values such as "racket-face angle" have no reference; sensor-only orientation also drifts over time.
  • Swing-type classification is unreliable: in a free-hitting drill, 197 swings came out as 65 serves, 8 forehands, 4 backhands and about 120 unclassified.
  • The synchronized timeline had no video frames: the camera on the courtside computer does not send frames back to the browser, and the software does not interpolate missing pose frames.
  • One camera at 14–16 frames per second with 81–82% tracking confidence; angles marked with an asterisk are image-plane angles, not three-dimensional joint angles.

What the recording establishes

The full pipeline runs live on court: sensor → wireless → computer → browser chart at about 500 Hz; camera → landmarks → angle labels; swing detection and phase assignment on real swings; a session report at the end.

What it does not establish

That any number is accurate; that the phase boundaries are in the right place; that the scores or feedback messages mean anything for coaching; or that using the system changes how anyone plays. None of that has been validated.

Validation still needed

Record and label real sessions swing by swing (myself and teammates) and tune detection and scoring on that data instead of the simulator; calibrate the sensor’s mounting orientation to separate forehand from backhand; add video tracking of the racket and ball to get the real racket-head path and contact point, then compare it with the sensor’s estimates; and finally a small multi-week comparison of players with and without the interface. None of this has started.

04 /DEVELOPMENT AND MY PART IN IT

What I built,
and what broke along the way.

The photos are in order, each with what it shows. After them: two documented changes, and what I did versus what I reused or had help with.

Two documented changes

Both come from my account in the introduction video, laid out as problem → change → why → next test → still open.

A

Clock alignment

Problem
The sensor, the computer and the browser each keep their own clock, and wireless data arrives late or goes missing. Early on, the racket peak and the matching video frame could be off by a noticeable fraction of a second — useless for any timing analysis.
Change
Align the clocks on the fastest delivery observed, correct the offset a little at a time as it drifts, and display the leftover uncertainty in the interface.
Why this approach
The fastest delivery is the closest estimate of the true offset; gradual correction avoids jumps in the chart; showing the uncertainty tells the user which timing conclusions to trust.
Next test
In the recorded session the interface reported sync 89% with a 0 ms offset.
Still open
The real error between a video frame and the sensor peak has not been measured, and the latency card still shows the raw clock offset.
B

Swing detection

Problem
The first detector used fixed speed thresholds. It worked on fast swings and felt nothing on slower, more compact ones.
Change
Detect sustained acceleration instead, then assign the phases afterwards from the moments where the racket changes direction.
Why this approach
A threshold-free rule generalises across swing sizes. The lesson I took from it: always test on data you did not build it for.
Next test
In the recorded session, 197 swings were detected over 22 minutes with phase chips and shading.
Still open
Detection and scoring were tuned on a simulator; there is no manual count to check misses and false detections; swing-type classification is unreliable.

What I did, and what I borrowed

This was not built by one person from nothing. Here it is split into what I did, what I reused, and the help I had.

What I did

  • Assembled the sensor unit, its housing and mounting; installed the camera unit on court.
  • Firmware for the sensor’s microcontroller: reading the IMU, a packet format that can tell when data is lost or corrupted, and the wireless link to the USB receiver. It was my first time programming a microcontroller.
  • Sensor-data processing: separating gravity from the swing acceleration, deciding when to smooth a signal, and estimating the racket’s orientation (and understanding why that estimate drifts).
  • The clock-alignment scheme, the swing detector and the phase logic.
  • The training interface: live view with skeleton, metric cards, live chart, synchronized timeline and session report.
  • The court tests and the two changes above.

What I reused

  • Body landmarks come from Google’s MediaPipe Pose model; the code that turns landmarks into angles, paths and phases is mine.
  • Wireless uses Espressif’s ESP-NOW protocol.
  • The interface and charts are built on open-source web libraries.
  • All hardware is off-the-shelf: ESP32-S3 boards, an ICM42688 IMU module, the ROCK 5C single-board computer, a camera and a suction mount.

Help I had

  • Teammates took part in the court tests.
  • A teacher advised on the project.
  • Parts of the code were written with AI coding assistants; the debugging, the trade-offs and the repeated re-testing were mine. In the video I call this “vibe coding”.
05 /SPECS AND FAQ

What using it
is actually like.

Only what is known. Where a quantity has not been measured (weight, size, battery life, setup time) this says so instead of giving an estimate.

What is inside the sensor?
An ESP32-S3 microcontroller board and an ICM42688 six-axis IMU (three-axis accelerometer plus three-axis gyroscope) in a short cylindrical housing at the butt end of the grip. Weight and dimensions have not been measured, so they are not published.
How is it attached, and which rackets does it fit?
It is fixed to the end of the grip, below where the hand sits (see the photos). Only my own rackets have been used; no other models have been tried. Its effect on grip feel and racket balance has not been measured.
How is it powered, and for how long?
Battery powered. Battery life has not been measured.
What has to happen before a session, and how long does it take?
Hold the racket still with the sensor fitted so it can calibrate, then record a neutral pose in the interface. Setup time has not been timed. The recorded session skipped the neutral pose, which is why all orientation-based values in it lack a reference.
Where does the camera go, and what equipment is needed?
The ROCK 5C and the camera sit in one enclosure held to the court glass by a suction mount, positioned to see the whole player. The sensor’s USB receiver plugs into this computer. A laptop on the same local network opens the web interface.
Which functions work during training, and which are for later review?
Live: camera view with skeleton, joint-angle labels, phase chips, the live IMU chart, the metric cards and the rule-generated feedback messages. After the session: the report (scores, swing-type statistics, deep-analysis chart, suggestions) and the swing-by-swing synchronized timeline.
Known tracking limitations?
One camera at 14–16 frames per second; angles marked with an asterisk are image-plane angles; missing frames are not interpolated; tracking confidence was about 81% in the recorded session. Sensor-only orientation drifts, and forehand and backhand are not yet reliably separated.
Can anyone try it?
No. There is one prototype and no app or product to download. The repository below is the place to follow progress.
Where are recordings and data processed and stored?
The recorded setup ran entirely on the local network (the interface shows "LAN online", 局域网在线), with processing on the courtside computer; the report page states that fusion results are computed in the browser and stored by the backend on that computer.
06 /ABOUT LEON

Squash player · school team captain · built SquashSense

Leon.

Why I built it.

I have played squash for about ten years. I am a senior at a boarding high school in the United States and captain of its squash team this season. Most feedback in squash is someone’s impression of a swing that lasts a fraction of a second, and solo practice gives no feedback at all. I wanted a way to measure a swing — the nick shot I hit by luck, say — and then hit it again on purpose. SquashSense is my attempt at that: a sensor, a camera, and software that puts the two on one timeline.

What I learned: almost everything was new to me — my first microcontroller program, my first wireless link, my first time handling raw sensor data. The lesson that stuck: always test on data you did not build it for. The detector that worked on my fast swings failed on slow ones.

Written summary

What I say in the video: feedback in squash is mostly subjective, solo practice has none, and I wanted to quantify a swing so a good shot can be repeated. The system has three parts — a motion sensor on the grip (500 times a second), a camera watching the player (33 landmarks per frame) and software that puts both on one timeline. The software splits each swing into six stages (preparation, backswing, forward swing, impact, follow-through, recovery) and calls contact when a shock appears on the sensor while the racket is near peak speed. The hardest part was making a number trustworthy: three unsynchronised clocks, lost wireless packets, and a threshold-based detector that missed slow swings. Almost every technique was a first for me. Next is validating detection and scoring on labelled real sessions, adding video tracking of racket and ball, and running a small comparison study — and I am not sure yet how that will turn out.
